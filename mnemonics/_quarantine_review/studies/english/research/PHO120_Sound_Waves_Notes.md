# Acoustic Phonetics: Sound Waves

## 1. Introduction to Sound Waves
Sound originates from the motion or vibration of a sound source, such as a tuning fork. The result of this vibration is known as a sound wave. Most sounds we hear are not pure tones but complex tones.

## 2. Simple Sound Waves
Simple sound waves are mathematically modeled as sine waves. They are regular in motion and are referred to as periodic. 

Two central properties define the measurement of simple sound waves:
* **Frequency**: Measured in Hertz (Hz). It denotes the number of cycles of a sound wave per second.
    * *Visual/Auditory Cue*: The instructor shows a waveform and points out one cycle. If one cycle takes half a second, the frequency is 2 Hz.
* **Amplitude**: Denotes the maximum displacement of a sound wave in a cycle of movement (the distance from the rest point). It is directly related to the loudness of a sound wave.
* **Intensity**: Because the total sensation of loudness combines both frequency and amplitude, the term intensity is used. It is measured in decibels (dB) and refers to the overall loudness of a sound.

## 3. Complex Sound Waves
Most sources of sounds produce complex sets of vibrations, which are synthesized from a sufficient number of simple sound waves. 
* Speech involves complex sound waves because it results from the simultaneous use of many sound sources in the vocal tract.
* **Types of complex waveforms**:
    * **Complex periodic sound waves**: Regular repeating patterns. In speech, vowels are basically periodic.
    * **Complex aperiodic sound waves**: Irregular patterns. Consonants range from periodic to aperiodic.

## 4. Resonance and the Source-Filter Model
The vibration of a sound source is normally intensified by the body around it. This intensification is referred to as resonance. Each sound wave consists of a sound source and some sort of resonance.

### Fundamental Frequency (F0)
The complex sound wave created by a sound source (like vocal folds or a saxophone reed) is referred to as the fundamental frequency, or F0 (often called F-naught). 
* In speech, F0 is the result of vocal fold vibration.
* Depending on age, sex, and pitch, F0 varies between 50 and 500 Hertz.

### Harmonics and Formants
F0 is filtered (intensified or damped) by numerous parts of the resonating body (e.g., the vocal tract).
* **Harmonics**: The resulting bundles of resonance frequencies are integer multiples of F0. 
    * *Example*: On an oboe playing tone A, F0 is 440 Hz. The first harmonic F1 is twice 440 Hz (880 Hz), F2 is three times F0 (1320 Hz), and so on.
* **Formants**: In speech, by modifying the shape of the vocal tract, different clusters of harmonics are boosted or smoothed. These clusters, or precisely their peaks, are called formants (F1, F2, etc.).
* **Resonance Chambers**:
    * The **pharyngeal chamber** (pharynx) creates the first formant (F1).
    * The **oral resonance chamber** creates the second formant (F2).
    * *Note*: The formant pattern of a speech sound is the outcome of the whole vocal tract working as one resonance system.

### The Source-Filter Model
This model associates laryngeal action with vocal tract resonance.
* *Visual Cue*: The instructor displays a schematic showing the unmodified laryngeal source wave at the bottom, the first three formant peaks in the middle, and the modified spectrum at the top.
* When comparing the frequency spectrum for the vowel [a] (open) and the vowel [i] (close):
    * The laryngeal frequency spectrum (F0) remains unaffected.
    * The frequency spectrum that includes the vocal tract resonances is different.
    * For [a], the first two formants (F1 and F2) are relatively close together.
    * For [i], it involves a low F1 value but a very high value for the second formant (F2).
    * *Explanation*: The oral cavity for [i], which is responsible for the F2 value, is much smaller compared with the oral cavity involved in [a]. This explains the high F2 in [i] and the low F2 in [a].
