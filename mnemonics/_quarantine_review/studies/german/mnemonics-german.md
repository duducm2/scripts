## Street 1: Re-tuned Mouth Moves

![Street 1](images/1.png)

🟧 **[A] [Arachne]**

💡 Context: German l is lighter than English l.

Narrative: Arachne lands by a painted mouth model and taps right behind the upper front teeth. Next to her, Ada Lovelace tries an English "leap" and then copies the lighter German move. Arachne keeps repeating the small tip motion until it feels natural.

Conclusion: "In German only the tip of your tongue will come up and it reaches just behind your front upper teeth."

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiced_dental_and_alveolar_lateral_approximants

🟧 **[B] [bird of paradise]**

💡 Context: [ŋ] uses the back of the tongue and should not end with a hard g.

Narrative: A bird of paradise inflates its throat while reading "Singen [ˈzɪŋən]" and "Angst [aŋst]" from a wet chalkboard. It pushes the sound from the back of its mouth and stops cleanly, refusing to add an extra g at the end. Arachne claps when the stop is sharp and correct.

Conclusion: "Unlike some English dialects, German does not add a hard \"g\" sound at the end. It is Singen, not \"Sing-gen.\""
IPA: Singen [ˈzɪŋən]

🟧 **[C] [cat]**

💡 Context: [ʃ] in German pulls the tongue farther back for a sharper sch feel.

Narrative: A cat drags a long silver S on the ground until it stretches into [ʃ]. Then it pulls its tongue far back and says "Schuh [ʃuː]" with a tighter, darker hiss. The bird of paradise copies the move and the sound suddenly locks in.

Conclusion: "The tongue tends to pull back a little further into the mouth and so instead of English's 'sh' you get 'sch'."

🟧 **[D] [dragon]**

💡 Context: German uses glottal stops often before vowel-starting words.

Narrative: A dragon speaks in short pops: uh-oh, uh-oh, uh-oh, like tiny doors opening and closing in the throat. It starts each vowel word with a firm stop, so the rhythm becomes hard and staccato. The cat stomps in time and the whole street sounds more intense.

Conclusion: "German tends to be more intense than English and so they tend to use them all the time... It gives the whole language a neat kind of guttural feel."

🟧 **[E] [eagle]**

💡 Context: Clusters join two consonants into one fast combined move.

Narrative: An eagle juggles three glowing blocks: [tʃ], [pf], and [ts]. It snaps [t] into [ʃ], then [p] into [f], then [t] into [s], so fast that each pair feels like one motion. The dragon catches the blocks and shouts "Match," "Pfund [pfʊnt]," and "Zehn [tseːn]" as the crowd repeats.

Conclusion: "Clusters occur when two consonants are placed together."

## Street 2: Deep German Sounds

![Street 2](images/2.png)

🟧 **[F] [frog]**

💡 Context: German [r] is made deep in the throat with the back of the tongue.

Narrative: A frog leans back and makes a rough rolling sound from deep in the throat, almost like playful gargling. Johann Sebastian Bach points at the back of the mouth on a giant model and makes everyone copy slowly. The eagle bows because this is clearly a new sound spot.

Conclusion: "It's produced using the back of your tongue which pulls back all the way behind where you pronounce 'g'."

🟧 **[G] [goat]**

💡 Context: The ach-laut [x] is a tight back-of-mouth air sound, like controlled choking air.

Narrative: A goat arches its back and pushes air through a narrow gap near the uvula while tracing the word "Bach [bax]" in fog on a mirror. The frog taps the exact spot on the throat model and makes the gap smaller each time. The sound comes out rough, dry, and steady.

Conclusion: "Your tongue raises up all the way in the back behind 'g' and as that space gets smaller and smaller... you get 'ch'."

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_velar_fricative

🟧 **[H] [Hydra]**

💡 Context: The ich-laut [ç] is a hissing sound made farther forward from a y-like tongue position.

Narrative: Hydra starts with a clear y mouth shape, then lifts the middle of the tongue closer to the roof and lets air squeeze through in a thin hiss for "Ich [ɪç]." Everyone turns once, repeats the hiss, and remembers the full path.

Conclusion: "This last consonant is made in that exact same spot... you just sort of squeeze air through that space to make a kind of hissing sound."

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voiceless_palatal_fricative

# Topic: Shifted Placements and Fast Diphthongs

## Street 3: Shifted Placements and Rounded Vowels

![Street 3](images/3.png)

🟧 **[I] [imp]**

💡 Context: The long `[aː]` sound pushes the tongue slightly closer to the front of the mouth.

Narrative: Glossonauta walks in to watch as an imp holds up a giant "Thumbs Up" sign to say "mag [maːk]." The imp pushes his tongue closer to his front teeth and waves the big thumb at the jester.

Conclusion: "The tongue must be placed slightly closer to the front of the mouth, in the direction of English [æ]."

🟧 **[J] [jester]**

💡 Context: The `[yː]` sound takes a high front tongue position but rounds the lips.

Narrative: The jester leaps onto a theater stage, hissing "Bühne [ˈbyːnə]." He drops his colorful hat right onto the imp's sign. He keeps his tongue totally still while twisting his lips into a tight circle.

Conclusion: "Rounded vowels take the tongue position of a more or less familiar vowel, and then they round their lips without moving the tongue."

🟧 **[K] [kitten]**

💡 Context: The `[øː]` sound moves the tongue slightly down but keeps the lips round.

Narrative: A kitten spreads thick oil paint, or "Ölfarbe [ˈøːlˌfaʁbə]," all over the stage floor. It throws a blob of the slippery paint onto the lion's tail, rounding its lips while shifting its tongue slightly down.

Conclusion: "Start with [e] (tongue moved slightly down to front-center), keep it still, and round your lips."

🟧 **[L] [lion]**

💡 Context: The `[œ]` sound uses a mid-mouth tongue shape with rounded lips.

Narrative: Goku flies down and spots a glowing lion sitting inside a dark stone archway, acting as an "Öffnung [ˈœfnʊŋ]." The lion keeps its tongue perfectly still, rounds its teeth, and kicks a loose stone at the marmoset.

Conclusion: "Start with [ɛ], keep tongue still, and round your lips."

🟧 **[M] [marmoset]**

💡 Context: The `[ɔ]` sound starts in the back-center of the mouth with round lips.

Narrative: A marmoset sits on the shoulder of a friendly older uncle, saying "Onkel [ˈɔŋkl̩]" and "Bonn [bɔn]." It rounds its mouth tightly and leans over to snatch a piece of sweet cake right out of the kitten's paws.

Conclusion: "Start with the English vowel [ʌ] as in but (back-center of mouth), keep tongue still, and round your lips."

## Street 4: Drops and Fast Diphthongs

![Street 4](images/4.png)

🟧 **[N] [Neanderthal]**

💡 Context: The `[ʏ]` sound uses a slightly lower tongue position while keeping lips round.

Narrative: The Neanderthal chews on a tiny piece of cake, mumbling "Stück [ʃtʏk]." Angry about the stolen bite, he steps forward and swings his heavy club to smash the owl's desk.

Conclusion: "Start with [ɪ] (tongue slightly lower and further back than [i]), keep tongue still, and round your lips."

🟧 **[O] [owl]**

💡 Context: The unaccented `[ɐ]` sound is placed extremely close to the English `[ʌ]` vowel.

Narrative: An owl dressed as a strict teacher glares and says "Lehrer [ˈleːʁɐ]." It points a sharp wooden ruler at the Neanderthal, placing its tongue extremely close to the 'uh' sound position.

Conclusion: "Extremely close to the English [ʌ] vowel."

🟧 **[P] [panther]**

💡 Context: Germans transition to the second vowel in a diphthong much faster than English speakers.

Narrative: A panther rides a fast elevator at a tall building site, booming "Aufzug [ˈaʊ̯fˌtsuːk]" and "Bau [baʊ̯]." It drops down instantly, snapping the sound quickly, and blasts the Quetzalcoatl with a sudden, fast roar.

Conclusion: "One of the biggest differences between German and English diphthongs is that the Germans tend to go faster to the second vowel."

🟧 **[Q] [Quetzalcoatl]**

💡 Context: For the `[ɔʏ̯]` sound, the lips must stay tight and rounded all the way to the end.

Narrative: The Quetzalcoatl wraps around a cow to milk its udder, calling it "Euter [ˈɔʏ̯tɐ]." The feathered snake keeps its lips tightly rounded the entire time without relaxing, then happily splashes a bucket of milk all over the panther's elevator buttons.

Conclusion: "When you say boy, your lips relax at the end, whereas in German they stay rounded and tense the whole way through."

# Topic: Compound Stress and Vowel Length Rules

## Street 5: Compound Stress and Consonants

![Street 5](images/5.png)

🟧 **[R] [Rat]**

💡 Context: Compound words keep their original stress patterns, but one primary stress dominates the whole word.

Narrative: A giant Rat builds a signpost out of small wooden blocks that spell Wegweiser [ˈveːkˌvaɪzə]. Harry Potter tries to lift the blocks with his wand, but the Rat forces them together, making one heavy block stick out on top as the primary focus. The Rat uses this heavy block to build a wall, completely blocking the Vulture from landing on that side of the street.

Conclusion: "German likes to assemble words out of smaller words... when you stick these two word elements together, they hold onto their original stress patterns... but one word its stress will stick out over all the others."

🟧 **[S] [Skull]**

💡 Context: Voiced consonants harden into unvoiced sounds at the absolute end of a word.

Narrative: A floating Skull drifts around the street, completely ignoring the singing Unicorn. It grabs a soft, buzzing letter 'd' from the Rat's pile and places it at the absolute end of the painted word Hand [hant]. The moment the letter hits the end of the line, the Skull bites it, hardening it instantly into a sharp, unvoiced 't' sound.

Conclusion: "When a voiced consonant like d, z, b, v, and g shows up at the end of a German word, when it is final in that word, it turns into an unvoiced consonant: t, s, p, f, and k."

🟧 **[T] [Toucan]**

💡 Context: Word elements keep their strict pronunciation rules even when trapped inside compound words.

Narrative: A colorful Toucan swoops down and steals a hard 'k' sound from the Skull. It jams the hard sound right into the middle of a new round village structure, building Runddorf [ˈʁʊntˌdɔʁf]. Even though the piece is trapped in the middle, the Toucan squawks loudly at the Rat to show that the piece stays perfectly hard and unchanged.

Conclusion: "German words basically never change how they sound even when they're just word elements jammed into the middle of a compound."

🟧 **[U] [Unicorn]**

💡 Context: To pronounce a short vowel, move to the consonants early and hold them.

Narrative: A Unicorn dressed as an opera singer stands next to the Toucan's new village. It holds a musical card reading Ass [as]. Instead of singing the vowel, the Unicorn quickly skips over the first letter and lands early on the double consonants, pressing its hooves down to stay on them a bit longer while ignoring the Vulture's noise.

Conclusion: "If you want to make the 'a' in 'Ass' short, just go to the consonants early and stay on them a bit longer." IPA: Ass [as]

🟧 **[V] [Vulture]**

💡 Context: Short vowels almost always come before two or more consonants.

Narrative: A heavily feathered Vulture lands beside the singing Unicorn, holding a sign with the word Bitte [ˈbɪtə]. It inspects the short vowel and aggressively guards the two thick consonants right behind it. The Vulture snaps its beak at the Skull, refusing to let any short vowel stand unless it brings at least two consonant guards.

Conclusion: "Nearly all short German vowels are followed by two or more consonants."

## Street 6: Vowel Length Rules

![Street 6](images/6.png)

🟧 **[W] [Wombat]**

💡 Context: Long vowels usually come before one or fewer consonants.

Narrative: On the next street, a Wombat meets Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart. Mozart plays a long, smooth note on his violin for the word Bote [ˈboːtə]. The Wombat pushes away the extra building blocks on the ground, ensuring the long vowel only has one single consonant standing behind it.

Conclusion: "Most long German vowels are followed by one or fewer consonants."

🟧 **[X] [Xena, warrior woman]**

💡 Context: The letter 'h' acts as a vowel lengthener.

Narrative: Xena, warrior woman, throws her sharp circular blade to carve the letter 'h' onto a wooden post for the word Ehe [ˈeːə]. The Wombat tries to add more consonants to the post, but Xena stretches the 'h' out like a long rope, magically pulling and lengthening the vowel sound before it.

Conclusion: "The letter h acts as a vowel lengthener."

# Topic: Consonant Limits and Vowel Reductions

## Street 7: Consonant Limits and Vowel Reductions

![Street 7](images/7.png)

🟧 **[Y] [yak]**

The /z/ sound never starts a word in Standard German.

A yak stands at the corner of the street. It holds a large sign with the word Symbol [zʏmˈboːl]. It tries to make a buzzing sound at the very beginning, but a giant red "X" stops it. A spiky-haired martial artist steps in to help the yak, showing that the buzz cannot start the word.

"The alveolar fricative /z/ never occurs word-initially in Standard German. A word like "symbol" is pronounced with a voiced alveolar fricative: Symbol." IPA: Symbol [zʏmˈboːl]

🟧 **[Z] [Zeus]**

The /ŋ/ sound only happens at the end of syllables.

Zeus throws a lightning bolt at the yak's sign, breaking it into pieces. He grabs a singing block, representing the /ŋ/ sound, and forces it to sit at the absolute tail end of the broken piece. He yells at the yak that the sound is trapped at the end.

"The velar nasal /ŋ/ only occurs at the end of syllables (syllable coda)."

🟧 **[Aa] [aardvark]**

Long vowels get short when they are not stressed.

An aardvark ignores Zeus and pulls on a long rubber band that says Moral [moˈʁaːl]. It pulls out another band that says Metan [meˈtaːn]. Because the aardvark is very relaxed and not stressed, it lets both bands snap back so they become very short.

"Most long vowels can be shortened when they appear in an unstressed position (e.g., Moral, Metan)." IPA: Moral [moˈʁaːl], Metan [meˈtaːn]

🟧 **[Ab] [Abyssinian cat]**

The weak 'e' sound can be completely dropped.

An Abyssinian cat plays with the aardvark's snapped bands. It finds a soft, weak letter 'e' in the word großem [ˈɡʁoːsəm]. The cat pushes the weak letter into a hole in the street, dropping it entirely so it vanishes.

"In words like großem, the schwa can be dropped entirely." IPA: großem [ˈɡʁoːsəm]

🟧 **[Ac] [acorn]**

The 'r' can be rolled loudly in southern areas or in singing.

A giant acorn rolls out of the hole the cat made. It bounces fast on the front of a tongue model, rolling loudly. The acorn wears a southern alpine hat and sings a loud opera song to the cat and the yak.

"Alveolar trill: Mostly used in southern dialects (Bavarian, Franconian) and in singing."

## Street 8: The German R Variations

![Street 8](images/8.png)

🟧 **[Ad] [adder]**

The 'r' can vibrate far back in the throat as a flexible option.

On the next street, an adder snake slithers around freely. It meets a tall villain in black armor, who points to the deep back of his throat. The snake vibrates its tail far back on a throat model, sliding around as a free and flexible option.

"Uvular trill: Can be used as a free variant of the voiced uvular fricative."

🟧 **[Ae] [aerialqist]**

The 'r' can be a friction sound pushed more to the front.

An aerialist swings on a trapeze above the snake. She swings her body forward to reach the front part of the villain's throat model. She rubs the surface to make a smooth, vibrating friction sound that sits more to the front.

"Voiced velar fricative: A more fronted variant."

🟧 **[Af] [Afghan hound]**

When 'r' is at the end, it opens up into a vowel.

An Afghan hound sits at the end of the street. It barks at the aerialist and the snake, holding a sign that says Mutter [ˈmʊtɐ]. Since the 'r' is at the absolute end of the word, the dog opens its mouth low and wide in the center, barking out a vowel sound instead of a hard consonant.

"Low central vowel /ɐ/: In syllable-final position, the orthographical 'r' is often realized as a vowel, as in Mutter." IPA: Mutter [ˈmʊtɐ]

🟦 **Additional notes (non-mnemonic)**

The main difference lies in **how air is obstructed** (manner of articulation) and **where it happens** (place of articulation).

Here is how these three sounds differ in production:

---

🟦 ### 1. Voiced Uvular Fricative [ʁ]

- **Where it happens:** At the uvula (the small dangle of tissue at the back of your soft palate).
- **How it is made:** You bring the back of your tongue very close to the uvula, creating a narrow gap. When air passes through, it generates a turbulent, continuous friction sound. Your vocal cords vibrate throughout.
- **Acoustic feel:** A smooth, continuous buzzing or friction sound at the back of the throat (common in modern standard French or German _R_).

🟦 ### 2. Voiced Uvular Trill [ʀ]

- **Where it happens:** At the uvula.
- **How it is made:** The back of your tongue is held loosely against the uvula. Instead of a steady narrow gap, the airstream forces the uvula to vibrate rapidly back and forth against the tongue. Your vocal cords vibrate.
- **Acoustic feel:** A rapid, rolling or vibrating sound at the back of the throat (like a traditional "gargling" _R_ sound).

🟦 ### 3. Voiceless Glottal Fricative [h]

- **Where it happens:** At the glottis (the space between your vocal cords deep in the throat).
- **How it is made:** Your vocal cords do not vibrate. Instead, they narrow slightly while air moves out from the lungs, or they simply adopt the shape of the following vowel sound without vocal cord vibration.
- **Acoustic feel:** A pure stream of unvoiced breath or aspiration (like the "h" in _hut_).

---

🟦 ### Comparison Summary

| Sound                 | IPA Symbol | Vocal Cords | Action Type           | Location |
| --------------------- | ---------- | ----------- | --------------------- | -------- |
| **Fricative**         | [ʁ]        | Voiced      | Narrow friction       | Uvula    |
| **Trill**             | [ʀ]        | Voiced      | Rapid vibration       | Uvula    |
| **Glottal Fricative** | [h]        | Voiceless   | Pure breath/narrowing | Glottis  |

Topic: Acoustic Phonetics and Sound Waves

Street 9: Simple and Complex Sound Foundations

![Street 9](images/9.png)

🟧 [Ag] [Agaric fungi]

💡 Context: Simple waves move evenly and repeat in a regular pattern.

Narrative: The Agaric fungi jiggles and bounces up and down in identical, repeating loops. Its smooth, regular jiggles completely ignore the angry shouts from the Greek warrior.

Conclusion: "Simple sound waves are regular in motion and are referred to as periodic."

🟧 [Ah] [Ah!—a sigh]

💡 Context: Amplitude shows how far a wave moves away from its resting point.

Narrative: A massive, floating word bubble that says "Ah!—a sigh" bites onto a thick rubber band attached to the giant mushroom. It pulls the band as far back as possible before letting go. The extreme distance the band stretches from the center shows its raw power, knocking Ajax's stopwatch onto the pavement.

Conclusion: "The amplitude of a sound wave denotes the maximum displacement of a sound wave in a cycle of movement."

🟧 [Ai] [Airedale terrier]

💡 Context: Real sounds are usually made of messy, overlapping vibrations.

Narrative: An Airedale terrier thrashes wildly in a puddle next to the mushroom. Instead of a smooth splash, the terrier creates a messy, overlapping crash of water drops. An Akita dog tries to grab the water, but the crazy vibrations spray everywhere.

Conclusion: "Most sources of sounds produce complex sets of vibrations."

🟧 [Ak] [Akita (dog breed)]

💡 Context: Vowel sounds are even and smooth, while consonants can be messy and irregular.

Narrative: An Akita dog floats above the street, howling smooth, perfectly repeating vowel notes. Then, the dog throws handfuls of rocks at Ajax's glowing throat map, making harsh, irregular consonant crashes. The smooth howling clashes directly with the messy rock noises.

Conclusion: "Vowels for example are basically periodic whereas consonants range from periodic to aperiodic."

Street 10: Formants and Resonance
![Street 10](images/10.png)

🟧 [Al] [alligator]

💡 Context: A sound gets louder when the space around it boosts the vibration.

Narrative: On the second street, a large alligator hisses softly, but it sticks its head inside a hollow metal barrel. The empty barrel catches the small sound and blasts it outward, making the hiss incredibly loud and intense. A floating angel covers its ears to block the noise.

Conclusion: "The vibration of a sound source is normally intensified by the body around it. This intensification is referred to as resonance."

🟧 [Am] [amulet]

💡 Context: The absolute base wave made by the source is the fundamental frequency.

Narrative: A glowing magical amulet pulses heavily, creating a deep, booming thud. The amulet sprouts tiny hands, grabs a red marker, and paints a giant "F0" on the side of the alligator's metal barrel, claiming that its pulse is the core, foundational wave of all the noise.

Conclusion: "The complex soundwave created by a sound source... is referred to as the fundamental frequency or F naught."

🟧 [An] [angel]

💡 Context: Resonance bundles multiply the base frequency to create speech formants.

Narrative: The angel floats near a glass tank. For every one pulse the amulet makes down the block, the angel multiplies itself into two, then three, then four angels. An eccentric scientist with wild white hair points at the multiplying angels and calls them bundles of formants.

Conclusion: "The resulting bundles of resonance frequencies or harmonics are multiples of F naught. In speech they are called formants."

🟧 [Ao] [aoudad]

💡 Context: The fundamental pitch comes directly from the vocal folds buzzing.

Narrative: A wild aoudad (mountain sheep) curls into a tight ball, buzzing and vibrating exactly like human vocal cords. The scientist places a speed meter on the sheep's horns, watching the buzz jump rapidly between fifty and five hundred ticks. The strong vibration shakes the glass tank where the angels are floating.

Conclusion: "In speech F naught is the result of vocal fold vibration. Depending on age, sex and pitch it varies between 50 and 500 Hertz."

🟧 [Ap] [ape]

💡 Context: The throat shapes the first formant, the mouth shapes the second, and their interplay with the base sound forms the source-filter model.

Narrative: A giant ape opens its jaws wide, holding a giant coffee filter. It swallows a glowing orb deep into its throat to create a low rumble (the first formant), and holds a second orb in its mouth for a higher hiss (the second formant). It then drops the buzzing aoudad (the source) through the coffee filter, watching the sound echo and change as it mixes the source and filter together.

Conclusion: "The pharyngeal chamber creates the first formant F1 and the second chamber is the oral resonance chamber which is associated with the second formant or F2. The interplay between F naught, the fundamental frequency, and its resonance frequencies has come to be known as the source filter model."
